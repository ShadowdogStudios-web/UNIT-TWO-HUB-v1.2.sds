Reconstruction's promise · 1865–1870

The moment

In 1870 a Black man rose to take his seat in the United States Senate — in the very chamber, and very nearly the very seat, once held by Jefferson Davis, the president of the Confederacy. His name was Hiram Revels, a minister from Mississippi, the first Black person ever to serve in Congress. A decade earlier, the Supreme Court had ruled in Dred Scott that Black Americans could never be citizens. Now a Black man was a United States senator, sent to Washington by voters who had been enslaved a few years before. White opponents argued that, because of Dred Scott, he had not been a citizen long enough to qualify. He was seated anyway, to cheering galleries.

This chapter is about that astonishing moment — the era called Reconstruction, when the United States tried, for the first time, to actually keep the promise of the Declaration. Historians now call it the “second founding,” because it remade the nation almost as profoundly as the Revolution had. The promise was nothing less than full citizenship and equality, written into the supreme law. The practice, from the very first day, was fierce and violent resistance determined to undo it. This chapter is the story of how high the country reached.

Freedom's First Work

When the war ended, four million freedpeople faced an enormous question: what would freedom actually mean? They answered it themselves, with a clarity that astonished observers. They searched for family members sold away years before, placing newspaper advertisements and walking hundreds of miles. They formalized marriages the law had never recognized. Above all, they hungered for two things: education and land.

The hunger for education was overwhelming — people forbidden under penalty of law to learn to read now built schools and packed classrooms, children and grandparents side by side. The federal Freedmen's Bureau helped distribute food, negotiate labor contracts, and establish schools. The hunger for land was just as deep, because land meant independence. Many believed, with reason, that they were owed it: a wartime order had briefly promised some “forty acres and a mule.” But that promise was almost entirely revoked, and most freedpeople were left to work the fields of the very people who had enslaved them. That failure would prove one of the most fateful of the era — political freedom without economic independence is a fragile thing.

Rewriting the Constitution

The greatest achievements of Reconstruction were three amendments that, together, remade the nation's founding promise. The Thirteenth (1865) abolished slavery. The Fourteenth (1868) is, many historians argue, the most important single addition ever made to the Constitution: it declared that all persons born in the United States are citizens — overturning Dred Scott at a stroke — and commanded that no state may deny any person “the equal protection of the laws.” That phrase is the legal foundation on which nearly every later civil-rights struggle would be built. The Fifteenth (1870) declared that the right to vote could not be denied on account of race.

The effect in the South was revolutionary. Protected by these amendments and, for a time, by federal troops, Black men registered and voted in enormous numbers — and they did not just vote, they governed. Some two thousand Black men held office across the South, from sheriffs and school-board members to state legislators, congressmen, and the senators Revels and Blanche Bruce. Black and white legislators together rewrote state constitutions and built the South's first public school systems. For a few years, a genuine interracial democracy was struggling to be born. It was fragile, incomplete, and under attack from the first day. But it was real, and it had never existed before.

Illinois: The Promise Reaches the Prairie

Reconstruction is usually told as a Southern story, but its promise reached the North too — including Illinois, where the fight for Black citizenship had a name: John Jones of Chicago. Jones was a free Black tailor who had arrived in 1845 with about three dollars and built one of the largest fortunes of any Black American of his time, turning his home into a station on the Underground Railroad where Frederick Douglass and even John Brown were guests.

But Jones's great cause was the repeal of the Illinois “Black Laws” — vicious statutes barring Black people from settling in the state, testifying against a white person, voting, or serving on juries. In 1864 he published a pamphlet against them and carried to the capitol a petition bearing eleven thousand signatures. He won: in that same extraordinary week of February 1865 when Illinois first ratified the Thirteenth Amendment, the legislature repealed the Black Laws. At the Springfield jubilee, Jones himself fired a cannon sixty-two times — once for each legislator who had voted for freedom. Five years later he voted for the first time, and in 1871 became the first Black person to hold public office in the history of Illinois.

    Activity 3 · Be the Historian

    How much really changed by 1870?

    Skill: Continuity & change over time — this rehearses the Part II writing on your Unit 2 exam.

    Reconstruction produced stunning headlines — Black senators, new amendments, interracial governments. But a historian has to ask how deep the change actually ran. Weigh the two kinds of evidence and take a position.

    The evidence of profound change
    Slavery was abolished; citizenship and the vote were guaranteed in the Constitution; two thousand Black men held office; the first public schools in the South were built. By the standard of where the country had stood in 1860, this was a transformation almost beyond imagining.
    The evidence of fragile change
    Yet most freedpeople had no land and remained economically dependent on their former enslavers. White Southern resistance was already organizing into terror. The federal commitment to enforcement was uncertain and would soon waver. The amendments were on paper; whether they would be enforced was another matter entirely.

    Consider: By 1870, had the lives of most freedpeople truly been transformed, or had the law changed while daily reality lagged dangerously behind? What is the difference between a right that exists on paper and a right that is actually protected?
