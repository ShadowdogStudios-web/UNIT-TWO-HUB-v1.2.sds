The Union Breaks

Secession, Sumter, and the start of the Civil War · 1860–1862

The moment

At half past four on the morning of April 12, 1861, a single shell rose over Charleston harbor, arced through the dark, and burst above a small island fort. With that flash of light, the argument became a war. For thirty-four hours Confederate guns pounded Fort Sumter until its commander surrendered. No one was killed in the bombardment itself — a strange, almost ceremonial beginning to the bloodiest war in American history. But the meaning was total. The United States had been fired upon. The fire bell Jefferson heard ringing forty years earlier was now a cannonade.

This chapter is about how the most carefully built government in the world came apart — and about the gap, one last time, between what Americans told themselves and what was actually true. The South said it was fighting for liberty and self-government. Its own leaders said something more specific. Recovering what they actually said, against the comforting myths that grew up later, is one of the most important things this unit asks of you.

A Nation Dissolves

Lincoln won the presidency in November 1860 without a single Southern electoral vote. He had promised not to touch slavery where it already existed, but he was firmly against its expansion, and that was enough. South Carolina seceded first, in December 1860, before Lincoln had even taken office. Within weeks Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed, and in February 1861 the seven formed the Confederate States of America, with its own president, Jefferson Davis — the same man who, decades earlier, had served in the Black Hawk War alongside a young Abraham Lincoln.

Secession was breathtaking: the South was declaring that the United States was not a permanent nation but a voluntary club any member could quit after losing an election. Lincoln rejected this absolutely — the Union was older than the states and could not be broken by one section's unhappiness. When the Confederates fired on Sumter, four more states — Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Arkansas — joined rather than send troops against fellow Southerners. The “more perfect Union” had failed its ultimate test: it could not peacefully contain the contradiction it had been built around.

Why They Said They Were Leaving

For more than a century afterward, many Americans explained the war as a noble struggle over “states' rights” in which slavery was only incidental. This explanation has a name — the Lost Cause — and it is contradicted by the documents the secessionists themselves wrote at the time.

When the seceding states explained their reasons, in official declarations of causes, they were remarkably direct: they were leaving to protect slavery. Mississippi's declaration stated flatly that its position was “thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery.” State after state named the protection of slavery as the central cause. And the Confederacy's own vice president, Alexander Stephens, removed all doubt weeks before the war: the old Union, he said, had rested on the mistaken idea that all men are created equal; the new Confederacy's “cornerstone” rested on the opposite truth — that slavery and the subordination of the Black race was the natural and proper condition of things. The “states' right” the South was defending was, above all, the right to own human beings.

Two Countries, Two Kinds of Strength

The two sides were profoundly unequal, though not in ways that guarantee victory. The North held more than twice the population, the vast majority of factories, railroads, banks, and shipping, the existing government, and the navy. The South did not need to conquer the North; it only needed to survive — to make the war so long and costly that the North would give up, the same strategy that had won independence from a stronger Britain. It fought on its own ground, and early on it had the better generals.

Both sides expected a short, glorious war. At the first major battle, Bull Run in the summer of 1861, civilians rode out from Washington with picnic baskets to watch the spectacle — and fled in terror as the reality of mass slaughter revealed itself. There would be no short war. There would be four years of it, and more than six hundred thousand dead.

The country had imagined a glorious afternoon. It had begun an apocalypse — a war that would kill more Americans than every other American war combined.

Illinois Goes to War

No state threw itself into the Union cause more fully than Illinois — Lincoln's own state, the prairie that had nearly become a slave state a generation before. Illinois sent roughly a quarter of a million men into the Union armies. At its southern tip, where the Ohio meets the Mississippi, the muddy town of Cairo became one of the most important Union bases in the country. And it was from Illinois that the war produced its greatest soldier: Ulysses S. Grant — the same man who had marched into Mexico and called that war unjust — was working in his family's leather-goods store in Galena, going nowhere, when the war gave him a purpose. The state that had come within two thousand votes of legalizing slavery in 1824 now stood at the very center of the war to destroy it.

    Activity 3 · Be the Historian

    What was the war really about?

    Skill: Sourcing — weighing documents against later memory — this rehearses the Part II writing on your Unit 2 exam.

    Few questions in American history have been argued more. The good news: the people who started the war left a written record of their reasons. Weigh that record against the story told later.

    The documentary record
    The secession declarations of Mississippi, South Carolina, Georgia, and Texas repeatedly and explicitly name the protection of slavery as their central cause. Stephens's “Cornerstone Speech” named slavery and racial inequality as the new nation's founding principle. The Confederate constitution even forbade its member states from abolishing slavery — hardly the act of a movement devoted to local choice.
    The Lost Cause version
    Written a generation later, it holds that the South seceded over states' rights and constitutional principle, with slavery a minor or incidental factor — a war about liberty and local self-government against federal tyranny. It became the dominant story in much of the country for a century.

    Consider: When the people who made a decision wrote down their reasons at the time, how much weight should those reasons carry against an interpretation invented later? And ask the follow-up the sources demand: states' rights to do what?
